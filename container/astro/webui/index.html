    reading…

    1Level the head

    When it's on, the head corrects for its own tilt from its attitude sensor, so deep-sky tracking doesn't need a perfectly level tripod — it just needs to know which way is down, which it does. Turn it off only if you've levelled the base by hand. It persists on the head and measurably changes tracking accuracy.

    Auto-level physically drives the head to level — do it before you align, never while tracking (it moves the head). With tilt compensation on you can usually skip it.

    2Align

    The head needs to know which way is north before it can point at a star. Three ways, best first:

    Solve the sky — most accurate, no compass

    Take a frame of the night sky and let the solver work out exactly where the head is pointing, then write that as the alignment. This is the dream path — it needs no compass and no phone sensors.

    Phone compass — if this browser is on a phone

    Hold the phone flat against the head, pointing the same way the camera does, and read its magnetometer. Only works on a phone, and a magnetometer is never as good as a solve.

    —
    Type a bearing — from a real compass
    °

    The true bearing the camera points at (magnetic compass corrected for declination).

    3Track

    Half rate splits the drift between sky and ground so each trails half as much — the landscape-astro compromise. Tracking needs the Astro Kit fitted and the head aligned; without the kit it can point and align but not track.

    4Auto-focus — tack-sharp stars

    Sweeps the focuser and settles where the stars are tightest — smallest HFR, scored from the live view at each step, exactly how NINA and Ekos focus. Put the camera on manual focus, aim at a starfield, and turn live view on first. The head reports no focuser position, so this is a relative sweep that returns from one side to take up backlash — run it again if a pass lands short.

    Sweep settings

    More steps or a larger step size widen the search; settle gives the lens and preview time between moves.

    5Go to a target

    Pick a deep-sky object and the head slews to it and tracks. Plate-solve align first, so it lands on target.

    Observatory bridges

    This server speaks three mount protocols at once, so most astronomy software can drive the Polaris directly: ASCOM Alpaca (auto-discovered — NINA, Stellarium), native INDI (KStars/Ekos, PHD2), and LX200 over TCP (Stellarium, SkySafari, or the LX200-generic driver). The INDI server exposes both a mount and a camera — connect the INDI Telescope and INDI CCD to this host and port; the camera returns a live-view frame for framing and plate-solving (full-res light frames are still shot through the Benro app).     Head settings

    Flips the camera-plate orientation (portrait ⇄ landscape). It moves the head a little; a small, safe re-orient.

    Off keeps the head's travel limits enforced — the safe default. Turn this on only to shoot the zenith or a ground angle with the Astro Kit, and only after you've physically checked the kit clears the pitch axis and the tripod. With limits released the head can swing into its own accessory.

    Watch the Astro Kit against the pitch axis and the tripod in real time — the head can collide.

    Wi-Fi

    The head keeps its own access point and also joins your network, so it is reachable without switching Wi-Fi. Both radios share one channel — joining forces the AP onto your network's channel.

    Sixty seconds after the last client disconnects the head powers its radio down and the network disappears. Holding the control link open prevents that, at the cost of battery — and, while the mount is not aligned, of making the Benro app ask for a compass calibration.

    Control link
